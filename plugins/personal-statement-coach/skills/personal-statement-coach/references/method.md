## 1. Definition & purpose
- **P1.** *"An essay written in the first person explaining why you want to study there and demonstrating why they would select you."* It answers two questions, and **why you** matters more.
- **P2.** The purpose is **to distinguish yourself.** Show personal characteristics *beyond the portfolio*, and show you are the exact slot they are looking for (a holistic fit).
- **P3.** It is the first thing read and the most important part of the application. Committees read **100+ a day**.
- **P4.** **The first line is the hook.** It's used once, but it can be tailored per application.
- **P5.** They want to know you **as a scholarship applicant, not as a person**. Be personal, not *too* personal.
- **P6.** It works like an **elevator pitch**: who you are, in 30 seconds.

## 2. Appealing to the reader
- **A1.** Make them feel you'll **fit in easily**.
- **A2.** Make them **trust you as if they had interviewed you**.
- **A3.** **Signaling:** concrete markers place you quickly. Examples: a recognised degree, an honour from an official, audience numbers, selective programs.
- **A4.** Give evidence that you are special and excellent.
- **A5.** **30% is what's in it; 70% is how you sound.** Your articulateness and tone carry your personality. *"It's like meeting someone without having to meet someone."*
- **A6.** **How you tell it beats what you did.** An ordinary volunteer role can be framed through its full ripple of impact: the environment, local income, the people who benefited downstream. Others who did the same work but tell it flatly are forgotten. See `examples.md` §7.

## 3. Writing process

### Part 1: Develop (7 items)
1. **Purpose.** *"Whoever has a why can answer any how."* Not tourism ("new friends, new food" means don't apply). The purpose must go **beyond yourself**.
2. **Research the institution:** professors, their awards, research and interests, rankings (QS first, then Times and Shanghai).
3. **Academic and career goals:** how the program shapes your future, your ultimate goal, **where you'll be in five years**, and the milestones on the way. Be **ambitious, not humble**.
4. **Why should *you* be chosen?** Research the scholarship's goals and tailor yourself to them. Cover qualities, the experiences that shaped you, your proudest accomplishments, and why you beat other candidates. Don't be humble, don't exaggerate, never lie. If an achievement is modest, show your role, its impact and what it taught you about wider (socio-economic) issues.
5. **List formal achievements** as a story, often chronologically: degrees, awards, conferences, official recognition.
6. **Outline:** when and why you got interested in the field, what you love about it, why it matters, and your related experience.
7. **Challenges:** financial hardship, discrimination, disenfranchisement, disability, family or medical problems, tragedy, being first-generation, a marginalized community, a working-class background. These show your achievement is *not ordinary given where you started*. Example: an IELTS 8 from a public school counts for more than from an international school; compare rates, not counts.

### Part 2: Put it together
- **W1.** Read and answer **every** specific question asked.
- **W2.** Outline the structure, one theme per paragraph. For example: education → work → extracurriculars.
- **W3.** Write a **strong beginning**. Superlatives work: *"The most important moment in my life was…"*
- **W4.** Write a body that supports the opening.
- **W5 / G3.** Keep a **positive tone.** Write "Despite X, I did Y", never blame the system. Never *"I'm not sure but I think I will probably be a good fit."* Write instead *"I'm a responsible student who values and practices hard work… I will…"*

### Part 3: Revise
- **R1.** Expand it if it's short; trim it if it's long.
- **R2.** **Read it aloud**, walking around.
- **R3.** Get feedback, ideally from someone who won that scholarship.
- **R4.** Proofread **at least twice**.

## 4. Must-include
- **M1.** A clear **motive** (what you want).
- **M2.** **Extracurriculars**, to show you're not only studying.
- **M3.** **Future plans.**
- **M4.** **Why this university or program** (and country), with researched specifics.
- **M5.** **Respect the limits:** page and word count.
- **M6.** **How the scholarship helps you afterwards**, and how you'll give back.

## 5. The 10 don'ts
1. **D1.** Repeating achievements or portfolio content (CV dumping).
2. **D2.** Being too personal: hobbies, cafés, the beach, your fiancé, favourite food.
3. **D3.** Self-promotion without substance, negativity, opinions, jokes, exaggeration.
4. **D4.** Leaving things unexplained.
5. **D5.** Opening with a quotation or definition. This one is debatable: it works only if the quote truly reflects your whole life and the essay builds on it.
6. **D6.** Clichés and motivational-poster slogans.
7. **D7.** Big dictionary words.
8. **D8.** The passive voice. Prefer active.
9. **D9.** Buying the PS or having AI write it. Programs check, and Chevening forbids it. AI is fine for the CV and a recommendation-letter draft.
10. **D10.** Ignoring the guidelines or word count.

## 6. The 10 tips
1. Brainstorm on blank paper; get everything out.
2. Know everything about the scholarship. Research professors on LinkedIn, the program's social media for the last 3-4 years, the whole website and its blogs, alumni on YouTube and LinkedIn.
3. Ask yourself many questions.
4. Arrange your answers in points.
5. **Self-awareness.** You learn who you are by moving and dealing with people ("الحركة بركة", movement is a blessing), not by sitting still.
6. Use a range of words and synonyms.
7. Split it into introduction, body and conclusion.
8. Avoid plagiarism.
9. **STAR technique.**
10. Revise the grammar.

## 7. The 29 feedback mistakes (real applicant errors)
1. Too much "I". Vary the subject: *"The fellowship selected me…"*
2. Opening with a quote. If you keep one, rephrase it into yourself: *"Failure has always empowered me to get up again and start over."*
3. An opening that's too short, or paragraphs that are too long.
4. The opening doesn't connect to the next paragraph.
5. Assuming the panel knows things. Explain your organisation, acronyms and local context.
6. Missing conjunctions, or no spacing between paragraphs.
7. "Thank you" anywhere.
8. Writing in the third person, like a bio.
9. "I am looking forward to hearing from you."
10. "To the X scholarship…" or "I'm writing this to…" as the opening.
11. "All of this makes me an excellent candidate."
12. Any Arabic words or Arabic script.
13. Future plans that aren't SMART.
14. Not stating your development after each extracurricular.
15. Obvious facts used as filler ("the USA is the third most populous…").
16. Giving advice or preaching.
17. Starting with "My name is…".
18. "Best regards" at the end.
19. The wrong structure. Use about 4 paragraphs unless told otherwise.
20. "I hope you like this personal statement."
21. Flat "I'm interested in…" openings.
22. Spelling and grammar errors.
23. No visible passion.
24. Not saying how the scholarship helps you afterwards.
25. Restating the question before each paragraph.
26. Numbered paragraphs.
27. A one-sentence paragraph.
28. A lowercase "i".
29. Skipping the final pass: read aloud, grammar check, feedback, scan, confirm the structure.

## 8. Frameworks
- **STAR:** **S**ituation (time, place, context, problem) → **T**ask (what you were theoretically meant to do) → **A**ction (what you practically did) → **R**esult (numbers). The order is flexible, but cover all four.
- **SMART:** Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant (to your values and long-term objectives), Time-bound.
- **V-SPICE:** **V**ulnerability (be honest about struggles), **S**elflessness (caring for others), **P**erseverance (kept going), **I**nitiative (started or led something), **C**reativity (love of learning, asking questions), **E**xpression (genuine, be yourself).
- **SDGs:** the UN's 2030 Agenda (adopted 2015). Link your purpose to them where it's honest.

## 9. PS vs motivation letter, length, non-native writers
- **PS:** for university applications; focuses on *why me*. **Motivation letter:** broader (scholarships, jobs, internships); focuses on *why this opportunity*. They are largely synonyms.
- **Length:** usually 650-1,000 words, about one page, unless the program sets a limit. Chevening is **300 words per essay** (it was 500).
- **Non-native writers:** *"Write clearly and honestly; you don't need fancy English. Admission officers care more about your story."*
- **Put the reader straight into your story.** Make it memorable among thousands.

## 10. Honesty, holistic review, recycling
- **H1.** **The past is 100% true. The future: dream freely.** No one holds you to a dream.
- **H2.** **Don't force it.** Don't write someone you aren't. The essay shows how you think.
- **H3.** **Holistic review.** The whole application is read together, so distribute the story across the PS, the motivation letter and the activities. Avoid CV dumping.
- **H4.** **Recycling.** Core paragraphs move between applications (for example, a scholarship essay reused for two university statements) with updated numbers and program-specific closes. Tailor the "why this program" and closing parts every time.
- **H5.** Get feedback once the first draft is done.
