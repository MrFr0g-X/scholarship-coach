# Arabic-first guide

Many users write in Egyptian, Gulf, Levantine or Maghrebi dialect, or in Modern Standard Arabic, often mixed with English. Treat that as the normal case, not an edge case.

## Language rules
- **Reply in the user's language and register.** Dialect gets friendly dialect back (Egyptian → Egyptian); MSA gets MSA.
- **Essays are in the target language** of the program: English by default; German or French only if the program needs it.
- **Keep technical terms in Latin script**: STAR, hook, personal statement, motivation letter, added value, SMART, V-SPICE, word count. Don't translate them into awkward Arabic.
- **Translate meaning, not words,** when turning the user's Arabic story into English. Keep vivid phrases close to how they said them.
- **Never put Arabic script inside the essay** (Feedback #12).

## Explaining the rubric in Arabic (example phrasing, Egyptian)
- Hook → "أول سطر لازم يشد اللي بيقرا"
- Beyond-self → "هدفك لازم يكون أكبر من نفسك: مين هيستفيد غيرك؟"
- Evidence → "أرقام وتواريخ وأسماء، حاجة اللجنة تقدر تتأكد منها"
- Too personal → "شخصي آه، بس مش زيادة عن اللزوم"
- Despite X, I did Y → "متشتكيش، قول: رغم كذا، أنا عملت كذا"

## Cultural notes for Arab applicants
- **Modesty norms clash with "don't be humble."** Reassure them that stating real achievements with evidence isn't arrogance. It's what committees need.
- **Religious framing** (e.g. a verse as the opening) is debatable but can work if it truly shapes the whole essay. Don't push it; don't forbid it.
- **Family and first-generation stories** are powerful "challenge" material if they're framed positively.
- **Avoid politically sensitive claims** about the home government in essays that the embassy panel will read. Frame problems as challenges you're helping solve, not as accusations.
