# Voice Guide: Sounding Like One Real Person

Committees read hundreds of statements. Most blur together because they're written in the same "application voice": abstract, inflated, symmetrical, and interchangeable. The ones that stick sound like **one specific person** telling a true story with details only they know. That's the target, whether you're drafting or reviewing.

## 1. Capturing the user's voice (before drafting)

Collect at least one sample:
- an old essay,
- emails or LinkedIn posts,
- WhatsApp-style explanations,
- or a voice-note transcript.

If there's nothing, ask two casual questions and use the answers:
- "Tell me, like you'd tell a friend, about the project you're proudest of."
- "Why do you actually want this, in your own words?"

Note from the sample:
- **Vocabulary level.** Which words do they use naturally? Don't upgrade "help" to "facilitate" unless they would.
- **Sentence rhythm.** Do they write short and punchy, or long and flowing? Match it, then vary it a little.
- **Their phrases.** Recurring expressions, the way they open a point, what they emphasise.
- **What they get excited about.** Put the energy where they put it.
- **Details they mention unprompted.** Names, places and numbers. Those are gold.

## 2. What real voice looks like
- **Concrete over abstract.** Not "I developed strong leadership skills" but "I had to convince 12 volunteers who were older than me to show up at 6 a.m."
- **Specific nouns.** The children's ward, the student union, the night shift, 150 volunteers.
- **Varied rhythm.** A long sentence that builds, followed by a short one. Like this.
- **Active verbs with a real subject.** "I sorted the donations," not "The donations were sorted."
- **An honest moment.** One line of doubt, struggle or surprise ("I knew nothing about advocacy") makes everything else believable.
- **A point of view.** One belief stated plainly and owned: "Nobody should need luck to get a good education."
- **Uneven paragraphs.** Real writing doesn't come in identical blocks.

## 3. Generic-prose patterns to cut (in drafts and reviews)

Flag each with the reason, then replace it with a concrete detail from the user.

| Pattern | Examples | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Template openers and closers** | "I am writing to express my interest", "Since childhood I have always been passionate about", "In today's fast-paced world", "In conclusion", "Thank you for considering my application" | Start in the story; end on others or the future. |
| **Inflated vocabulary** | delve, tapestry, testament to, pivotal, multifaceted, realm, navigate (the complexities), foster, leverage, holistic (as decoration), unwavering, profound, embark on a journey, ever-evolving, landscape | Use the plain word the user would say. |
| **Empty "-ing" tails** | "…, highlighting the importance of teamwork", "…, showcasing my dedication" | Delete the tail or show the result with a number. |
| **Claim without proof** | "I am a passionate and dedicated leader" | Replace with the story that proves it. |
| **Reflexive triplets** | "innovative, impactful, and sustainable", repeated in every paragraph | Keep one triplet at most; prefer one precise word. |
| **Mirror constructions** | "not only… but also…", "It's not just X, it's Y", used repeatedly | Say the point directly. |
| **Connector chains** | Furthermore… Moreover… Additionally… In addition… | Vary them, or let ideas connect naturally ("That's why", "So", "Then"). |
| **The lesson sentence** | "This experience taught me the value of perseverance." | Show what you did differently afterwards. |
| **Vague quantities** | many, numerous, various, a wide range of | Give the number, or cut it. |
| **Dash and colon tics** | an em-dash in every paragraph, "Here's the thing:" | Use plain punctuation mostly. |
| **Uniform paragraphs** | every paragraph 4 sentences of the same length | Vary them by content. |
| **Over-hedging** | "I believe that I might be able to…", "hopefully", "I'm not sure but…" | State it (Rule G3). |
| **Obvious-fact filler** | "The UK is home to many world-class universities." | Cut it (Feedback #15). |

## 4. Non-native writers
- Plain, correct, clear English **at their level** beats ornate prose. *You don't need fancy English; admission officers care more about your story.*
- Fix grammar and spelling. Don't replace their sentence structure with a native-speaker flourish they'd never produce. If a sentence wouldn't come out of their mouth in an interview, it's wrong for the essay.
- Idioms and phrasal verbs only if they already use them.

## 5. Quick self-test (after drafting or polishing)
1. **Read it aloud.** Any sentence you'd never say to a person? Rewrite it.
2. **The swap test.** Could this paragraph appear in another applicant's essay unchanged? Then it needs a detail only this person has.
3. **The interview test.** Could the user explain every sentence and number on the spot? If not, change it or ask them.
4. **The pattern scan.** Run `scripts/check_draft.py`. Its "generic phrasing" section lists hits from the table above.
5. **The rhythm scan.** Are sentence lengths varied? Do paragraphs differ in size?
